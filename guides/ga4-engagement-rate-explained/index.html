GA4 engagement rate, explained

Updated October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

In short: Engagement rate is the share of sessions that lasted longer than 10 seconds, had a key event or had two or more page views. Bounce rate is the rest. For a single page, read it in the Landing page report: page-level rates in explorations are inflated by visits that arrived from another page.

Engagement rate in GA4 is the percentage of sessions that were engaged. A session counts as engaged when it lasts longer than 10 seconds, includes a key event, or includes two or more page or screen views. If 400 of 1,000 sessions meet at least one of those conditions, the engagement rate is 40%.

The definition is simple. Reading it correctly, especially for a single page, takes a little more care. This guide covers what the metric counts, where to find it, how it relates to bounce rate, and the mistake that makes some pages look far more engaging than they are.

What counts as an engaged session

Google defines an engaged session as one that meets any of these three conditions:

  • It lasts longer than 10 seconds. Time is measured while the page is in the foreground, so a tab left open in the background does not keep adding seconds.
  • It has a key event. Any event you have marked as a key event, such as a sign-up or a purchase, makes the session engaged on its own.
  • It has two or more page or screen views. A visitor who opens a second page is engaged, however quickly they leave.

Engagement rate is engaged sessions divided by all sessions. GA4 reports the count as Engaged sessions and the share as Engagement rate, so you can always check the rate against the raw numbers behind it.

Engagement rate and bounce rate

In GA4, bounce rate is the percentage of sessions that were not engaged. The two metrics always add up to 100%. A page with a 62% engagement rate has a 38% bounce rate, by definition.

This is different from Universal Analytics, where a bounce was a single-page session with no further hits. A visitor who read a long article for four minutes and then left counted as a bounce there. In GA4, the same visit is engaged because it lasted longer than 10 seconds. Bounce rates in GA4 are therefore usually lower than the figures teams remember from Universal Analytics, and the two should not be compared directly.

Where to find engagement rate

Most standard reports do not show engagement rate by default. The Traffic acquisition report is the exception: open Reports > Acquisition > Traffic acquisition to see engaged sessions and engagement rate for each channel.

To see it in other reports, such as Landing page, an Editor or Administrator can customize the report:

  1. Open the report and click the pencil icon (Customize report) in the top right.
  2. Select Metrics, then Add metric, and choose Engagement rate. Add Bounce rate too if you want both.
  3. Click Apply, then save the changes to the current report.

The new metrics appear as the last columns of the table. Customizations apply to everyone who uses the property, so agree on them with your team. If you can't edit reports, a free-form exploration gives you the same metric without changing anything for others.

Reading engagement rate for a single page

Engagement rate is a session-scoped metric. It describes a whole visit, not a page. That matters as soon as you combine it with a page dimension, and it is the most common source of misleading engagement numbers.

Consider two ways to look at the rate for /pricing:

  • Landing page report. Each session is counted once, on the page where it started. The engagement rate for /pricing tells you how many visits that began on the pricing page went on to engage. This is the number to use when you want to judge the page as an entry point, for example for organic search.
  • Exploration with Page path. A session is counted for every page it included. Any session that reached /pricing from another page already has two page views, so it is engaged by definition. Pages that people usually reach mid-visit, such as pricing or checkout, can show engagement rates close to 100% here, regardless of how good they are.

A short example makes the difference clear. Suppose 200 sessions start on /pricing and 80 of them engage: the landing page engagement rate is 40%. Another 600 sessions arrive on /pricing from the home page. In an exploration, the page now has 800 sessions and 680 engaged ones, an 85% engagement rate. Both numbers are correct. Only the first one says anything about the pricing page as a first impression.

As a rule, use the Landing page report for engagement rate per page, and treat page-level engagement rates from explorations with caution.

What is a good engagement rate?

There is no single good number. Engagement rate depends on the type of page, the traffic mix and the device split. A help article that answers a question in eight seconds can do its job perfectly and still count as a bounce. A product page with a strong call to action will usually look better, because the second page view comes naturally.

Rather than chasing an external benchmark, set your own:

  • Compare a page with its own previous period, using the Compare option in the date picker.
  • Compare pages of the same type with each other, such as blog posts with blog posts.
  • Break the rate down by channel. Organic search and paid social visitors often behave very differently on the same page.

If your property has benchmarking enabled, GA4 can also show how your metrics compare with a peer group of similar businesses. Look for the benchmarking badge on metric cards in the Acquisition and Engagement reports. It requires the "Modeling contributions & business insights" data-sharing setting and enough data to form a peer group.

Changing the 10-second threshold

If 10 seconds is too short to mean anything for your content, you can raise it. In Admin, open Data streams, choose your web stream and select Configure tag settings. Under Settings, click Show all and then Adjust timer for engaged sessions.

The change only affects data collected after you save it. Make a note of the date, because engagement rate will shift on that day for reasons that have nothing to do with your pages.

Checking it without leaving the page

If you review pages one at a time, SideNote Analytics can show engagement rate and bounce rate in Chrome's side panel for the page you are on, next to the previous period. It is a quick way to spot a page whose engagement has changed before you open a full report.

Common questions

Why is my engagement rate so much higher than my old bounce rate suggested?

GA4 counts a single-page visit as engaged if it lasts longer than 10 seconds. Universal Analytics counted the same visit as a bounce. The metrics measure different things, so the jump is expected.

Can engagement rate be 100%?

Yes, especially in an exploration filtered to a page that people usually reach from another page. Every such session has at least two page views and is engaged by definition. Check the Landing page report for a more useful figure.

Does engagement rate include time in background tabs?

No. Engagement time is measured while the page is in focus. A tab that sits behind other tabs does not add engagement time.
